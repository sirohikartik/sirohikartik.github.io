FinAbstain: Do Small Open Language Models Know Why a Financial Question Cannot Be Answered?

Kartik Sirohi, Kumar Prateek, Simranjit Singh
Department of Information Technology, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar National Institute of Technology Jalandhar, Punjab, India
GitHub (SPELLAILab/FinAbstain) ↗

Motivation

Small open language models under 20B parameters can run locally on private hardware, which is useful when corporate financial filings cannot be sent to external APIs. In financial question answering, however, returning an unsupported or confident wrong number can be far worse than declining to answer. While existing benchmarks (such as FinQA and FinanceBench) focus heavily on answerable questions, they rarely evaluate whether models know when to abstain and why.

The FinAbstain Benchmark

FinAbstain is a benchmark of 143 questions constructed from the FY2024 SEC 10-K filings of four companies:

  • Amazon: 17 items
  • Caterpillar: 32 items
  • Coca-Cola: 42 items
  • Pfizer: 52 items

Each item consists of a question, an $\sim$8,000-token context padded with unrelated filing text, and one of four mutually exclusive gold labels:

  • Answerable (50 items): Exactly one verified value or statement in the text answers the question unambiguously.
  • Missing evidence (33 items): The passage discusses the general topic, but the requested metric was deleted from the text.
  • Conflicting evidence (38 items): The passage states two contradictory values for the same metric, entity, and period without reconciliation.
  • Ambiguous question (22 items): The question omits an essential qualifier (such as period or operating segment), leaving multiple valid answers in the text.

To avoid memorization from pre-training data, monetary amounts were randomly scaled by $\alpha \in [0.55, 1.8] \setminus [0.9, 1.1]$. Gemma 4 31B generated initial question-answer pairs and proposed surgical edits for unanswerable items. Edits were applied deterministically by code, verified through code checks, and double-checked through blind validation by two independent models (gpt-oss-120b and nemotron-3-super). An item was included only when both validator models agreed with the gold label.

The complete benchmark dataset and evaluation suite are available on GitHub at SPELLAILab/FinAbstain.

Evaluation & Results

We evaluated three open models under 20B parameters: Qwen3.5-9B, Granite-4.1-8B, and Phi-4 (14B) as Q8_0 GGUFs via llama.cpp on an NVIDIA H100 GPU slice using greedy decoding (temperature 0, seed 42) with a 16,384-token context window. Models were prompted to return a JSON object with their decision (answer or abstain), the reason (none, missing, conflict, ambiguous), and a short answer.

What Was Found

  • Answerable questions are handled reliably: All three models answer 49 out of 50 answerable questions (over-refusal is only 2.0%), achieving 91.0% to 94.7% answer token F1. When the evidence is present, small models extract it well.
  • Over-answering is widespread: On unanswerable questions, models answer 63.4% to 73.1% of the time. Overall decision accuracy (51.0% to 52.4%) falls below the 65.0% always-abstain baseline.
  • Missing evidence vs. conflict/ambiguity: Models detect missing evidence in 51.5% to 75.8% of cases. However, they almost completely fail to detect conflicting evidence (≤10.5%) and ambiguous questions (≤9.1%). Instead of flagging contradictions or missing qualifiers, small models latch onto the first number they encounter (primacy bias) and answer with confidence.
  • Human benchmark audit: A human annotator independently labeled a stratified subset of 44 items blind (without gold labels or edit logs). The annotator achieved 79.55% four-class accuracy, a Cohen's $\kappa$ of 0.7295, and 86.36% binary decision accuracy (recovering 8 of 10 conflict items and 10 of 16 ambiguous items). This indicates that the conflicts and ambiguities are readily visible to human readers even while small models overlook them.
